# Competitive Land Grab Plan

Assume a well-funded competitor enters. Money buys ads, engineers, and a slick app. It does not quickly buy trust, local relationships, or the habit of a recurring weekly game. Our land grab is to build the things money cannot rush, fast, in real cities, before anyone moves.

## Assets that are hardest to replicate

Ranked by how defensible they are:

1. The habit of recurring games. Once four people meet every Tuesday and it is part of their week, no app switch moves them. This is the deepest moat and it is exactly our North Star. Every recurring table we form is territory a competitor cannot easily take.
2. Trusted local relationships. The Oh My Mahjong network and the ambassadors are real people who vouch for us. A funded newcomer starts cold; we start warm. Relationships compound and do not transfer to a competitor.
3. A safety and trust reputation with older players. Our audience is cautious and values safety (public meetups, no shared contact info, a real person to call). Trust with seniors is slow to earn and easy to lose, which protects an incumbent who earns it first.
4. The founder's credibility as a certified instructor and the sister-site audience (lasvegasmahj.com). Authentic authority in the mahjong world is not purchasable at speed.
5. Density of real local activity (tables, teachers, ambassadors) in a city. A directory with real games in your town beats an empty national app, and density is slow to build city by city.

## The four moats

- Relationship moat: a named, local, trusted ambassador in each city. A competitor would have to find, win, and activate their own local leaders, one city at a time, against people who already trust us.
- Community moat: recurring groups that think of themselves as "our Tuesday game." Belonging is sticky. The group, not the app, is the product, and the group stays.
- Trust moat: the safety-first, senior-friendly reputation. Being known as the safe, kind place to find a game is a brand position a funded competitor cannot simply outspend, and any safety misstep by a rival reinforces us.
- Network effects: more players make tables fill faster, which attracts more players and more hosts; ambassadors bring players and teachers, teachers bring students, filled tables and the "need a 4th" wedge pull in the last players. Density in a city makes the next table easier, which a thin national competitor cannot match locally.

## How to become the default, before competitors move

The goal is to be the obvious answer to four questions in our cities, fast.

Find a game:
- Win the local search and the local word of mouth. Get real tables and the heat map working so demand routes to real games. Be the link every Facebook group admin pastes when someone asks "anyone playing near me."

Find a player:
- Make "need a 4th" the reflex. Every host short a player uses us because it actually fills the seat (priority referrals plus the want-to-play pool). The faster we fill seats, the more hosts default to us.

Find a teacher:
- Build the real teacher directory to the launch bar (20 real teachers) and make ambassadors the seed. Being the place beginners find a trusted local teacher is a category few will bother to build well, especially with safety baked in.

Start a table:
- Keep the one-minute, senior-friendly Start a Table flow as the easiest on the market, and put it behind every ambassador and teacher. Easy creation plus reliable filling is the loop.

## The land-grab sequence

1. Go deep before wide. Win the three proof and launch cities completely (ambassadors, teachers, recurring tables, a real city page) rather than spreading thin nationally. Depth is the moat; a thin national footprint is the thing a funded competitor can erase.
2. Lock the local leaders. Sign the best teacher, organizer, or group admin in each target city before a competitor can. Relationships claimed are relationships denied to rivals.
3. Build recurring games relentlessly. Recurring tables are the territory. Count them as the real land taken.
4. Own the safe-and-trusted position publicly. Make safety and warmth the brand so any aggressive, impersonal competitor looks worse by contrast.
5. Use the heat map to expand only where demand is already proven, so each new city starts with a waiting audience and an ambassador, not a cold launch a competitor could contest.

## What not to do

- Do not chase national vanity scale or a big ad push to look big. A funded competitor wins a spending war; they cannot win a trust-and-habit war. Spending to look big trades our real moat for their strongest ground.
- Do not weaken safety to grow faster. The trust moat is the one a competitor most wants us to abandon.
